============================================================ 1. GETTING
STARTED WITH MONEY IN THE U.S.
============================================================

1.1 THE U.S. FINANCIAL SYSTEM The U.S. financial system includes banks,
credit unions, lenders, credit-reporting companies, investment firms,
insurance companies, employers, and government agencies. Different
organizations do different jobs: banks and credit unions hold deposits
and provide payment services; lenders extend credit; investment firms
provide access to securities; insurers transfer specified risks; and
agencies such as the CFPB, FDIC, IRS, SEC, and FTC provide rules,
oversight, or consumer information.

1.2 IMPORTANT FINANCIAL DOCUMENTS An SSN is a Social Security number
used for Social Security records and many identification or tax-related
purposes. An ITIN is an IRS tax-processing number for certain people who
need a U.S. taxpayer identification number but are not eligible for an
SSN. A W-4 tells an employer how to calculate federal income-tax
withholding. A W-2 reports wages and taxes withheld from an employee,
while various Forms 1099 report certain kinds of non-wage income. Bank
statements and pay stubs help you verify transactions, income,
deductions, and balances.

1.3 IMPORTANT FINANCIAL TERMS Income is money received. Gross income or
gross pay is generally income before applicable deductions; net pay is
what remains on a paycheck after deductions. An asset is something of
economic value you own, while a liability is an obligation you owe. Net
worth is assets minus liabilities. Principal is the amount borrowed or
invested before interest. Interest is the cost of borrowing or a return
paid on certain savings. APR expresses certain borrowing costs as an
annual rate, while APY reflects interest earned on a deposit account and
the effect of compounding.

1.4 BUILDING YOUR FINANCIAL FOUNDATION A useful starting point is
knowing what comes in, what goes out, what you own, and what you owe.
Track income and recurring expenses, build savings for unexpected costs,
understand the terms of any debt, and choose goals that can be measured.
A budget is not a punishment; it is a plan for directing limited money
toward current needs and future priorities.

1.5 NEW TO THE UNITED STATES? You may encounter unfamiliar systems
involving credit histories, employer benefits, tax withholding, health
insurance, banking, and leases. You do not need to learn everything at
once. Start with immediate needs—safe banking, understanding your
paycheck and bills, protecting personal information, and learning how
credit works—then explore taxes, insurance, investing, and retirement as
they become relevant.

============================================================ 2. BANKING
============================================================

2.1 WHAT IS A BANK? A bank accepts deposits and may provide checking
accounts, savings accounts, payment services, and loans. Banks make it
easier to store money, receive direct deposits, pay bills, and move
funds. If a bank is FDIC-insured, eligible deposit accounts receive
federal deposit insurance within applicable limits.

2.2 BANKS VS. CREDIT UNIONS Banks and credit unions can both offer
deposit accounts, cards, and loans. Credit unions are member-owned
institutions and generally require membership based on their eligibility
rules. Federally insured credit unions generally receive deposit
insurance through the National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund
administered by the NCUA, while eligible deposits at FDIC-insured banks
are insured by the FDIC.

2.3 CHECKING ACCOUNTS Checking accounts are transaction accounts
designed for frequent deposits and withdrawals. They are commonly used
for direct deposit, debit-card purchases, checks, ATM withdrawals, and
bill payments. Compare monthly fees, overdraft policies, ATM access,
minimum-balance requirements, and interest before choosing one.

2.4 SAVINGS ACCOUNTS Savings accounts are designed to hold money you do
not expect to spend as frequently. They may pay interest, usually
expressed using APY. Savings accounts can be useful for emergency
savings, short-term goals, or money you want separated from everyday
spending.

2.5 OPENING A BANK ACCOUNT A financial institution generally verifies
identity and asks for information required by law and its own policies.
Requirements vary, so a newcomer should ask the institution which
identification and taxpayer information it accepts. Before opening an
account, review the fee schedule, minimum balance, ATM access, overdraft
options, interest or APY, and deposit-insurance status.

2.6 FDIC INSURANCE FDIC insurance protects eligible deposits if an
FDIC-insured bank fails. The standard insurance amount is $250,000 per
depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. Covered products
include checking accounts, savings accounts, money-market deposit
accounts, and CDs. Stocks, bonds, mutual funds, crypto assets,
annuities, and life-insurance policies are not FDIC-insured merely
because a bank sells them.

2.7 DEBIT CARDS A debit card normally accesses money in a linked deposit
account rather than borrowing from a credit-card issuer. Purchases and
withdrawals reduce available funds. Monitor transactions, protect the
PIN, report unauthorized activity promptly, and understand any overdraft
settings.

2.8 ATMS ATMs allow users to withdraw cash and may also support
deposits, balance inquiries, or transfers. Using an ATM outside your
bank’s network can result in fees from your bank, the ATM operator, or
both. Check the machine for signs of tampering and shield your PIN.

2.9 DIRECT DEPOSIT Direct deposit electronically sends wages or other
payments into a bank or credit-union account. It can reduce reliance on
paper checks and make funds easier to access. Verify routing and account
numbers carefully when setting it up.

2.10 BANK FEES Possible fees include monthly maintenance, out-of-network
ATM, overdraft, insufficient-funds, wire-transfer, and stop-payment
fees. Fee structures vary widely. Read the account disclosures and
compare accounts instead of assuming every checking account is free.

2.11 ONLINE AND MOBILE BANKING Online banking can let you check
balances, transfer funds, deposit checks, and pay bills. Use strong
unique passwords, enable multifactor authentication when available, and
avoid entering banking credentials through links in unexpected texts or
emails.

2.12 CERTIFICATES OF DEPOSIT A certificate of deposit, or CD, generally
pays interest in exchange for leaving money deposited for an agreed
term. Withdrawing before maturity can trigger an early-withdrawal
penalty. Compare APY, term length, penalties, and whether the bank is
FDIC-insured.

2.13 BANK ACCOUNT SAFETY Review statements and alerts regularly, use
official banking apps or websites, and protect login credentials. Be
skeptical of anyone asking for passwords, one-time security codes, gift
cards, cryptocurrency, or urgent transfers. If you see an unauthorized
transaction, contact the financial institution promptly.

============================================================ 3. CREDIT
============================================================

3.1 WHAT IS CREDIT? Credit lets you borrow money or obtain goods or
services now with an agreement to repay later. Common examples include
credit cards, auto loans, mortgages, personal loans, and student loans.
Borrowing can involve interest and fees, and repayment behavior can
become part of your credit history.

3.2 WHY CREDIT MATTERS Lenders can use credit information when deciding
whether to approve an application and what terms to offer. Credit
reports may also be used for certain other legally permitted purposes.
Having little or no U.S. credit history is not the same as having bad
credit; it may simply mean there is not enough reported information yet.

3.3 CREDIT REPORTS A credit report contains information about your
credit accounts and payment history. The three nationwide
credit-reporting companies are Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. Review
reports for unfamiliar accounts, incorrect balances or payment
histories, and other errors.

3.4 CREDIT SCORES A credit score is calculated from information in a
credit report to estimate credit risk. There are multiple scoring
models, so consumers can have more than one score. Income itself is not
a credit-score factor, and a credit score is not a measure of personal
worth or overall financial success.

3.5 FACTORS THAT CAN AFFECT CREDIT Scoring models can consider payment
history, balances relative to available credit, length of credit
history, account types, and recent applications. Exact formulas differ
by model. PennyWise should not promise that any action will raise a
score by a specific number of points.

3.6 BUILDING CREDIT FROM SCRATCH Possible starting tools include secured
credit cards and certain credit-building loans. Before opening a
product, compare fees, interest, deposit requirements, and whether
activity is reported to nationwide credit-reporting companies. Paying
obligations on time is central to establishing a positive history.

3.7 CREDIT CARDS A credit card is revolving credit. Important terms
include credit limit, current balance, statement balance, minimum
payment, due date, APR, fees, and any grace period. Paying only the
minimum can make repayment take longer and increase interest costs.

3.8 APR AND INTEREST APR expresses a borrowing rate on an annual basis,
although the exact cost depends on the account terms and how balances
are calculated. Cards can have different APRs for purchases, cash
advances, and balance transfers. Read the agreement instead of assuming
all transactions receive the same rate or grace period.

3.9 CREDIT UTILIZATION Credit utilization compares revolving balances
with credit limits. A $250 balance on a $1,000 limit equals 25%
utilization. Scoring models consider how close balances are to limits.
The often-repeated 30% figure is not a magical cutoff; lower use of
available revolving credit generally reduces this particular risk
signal.

3.10 SECURED CREDIT CARDS A secured card generally requires a refundable
security deposit that helps protect the issuer. It can be useful for
someone with limited or damaged credit, but it is still a credit card
with payment obligations, possible interest, and fees. Check reporting
practices before applying.

3.11 AUTHORIZED USERS An authorized user has permission to use another
person’s credit-card account but is not necessarily responsible for the
debt in the same way as the primary account holder. Reporting practices
vary by issuer. Becoming an authorized user should not be presented as a
guaranteed way to achieve a particular score.

3.12 HARD VS. SOFT INQUIRIES A hard inquiry commonly occurs when you
apply for credit and can affect a credit score. Soft inquiries can
include checking your own report and certain account reviews or
prescreening. Checking your own credit report does not hurt your score.

3.13 LATE PAYMENTS Payment history is an important credit-scoring
factor. Missing required payments can result in fees and can damage
credit history. Calendar reminders and autopay can help, but users
should still monitor balances and ensure enough money is available.

3.14 CHECKING YOUR CREDIT REPORTS AnnualCreditReport.com is the
federally authorized website for obtaining reports from the nationwide
credit-reporting companies. Checking your own report does not lower your
score. Review reports regularly for errors or signs of identity theft.

3.15 CREDIT REPORT ERRORS If information is wrong, dispute it with the
credit-reporting company and the business that supplied the information.
Clearly identify the item, explain why it is wrong, and provide
supporting documents when available. Keep copies of correspondence and
records.

3.16 HEALTHY CREDIT HABITS Pay required bills on time, keep revolving
balances manageable, avoid unnecessary applications, review reports, and
understand account terms. You do not need to carry an interest-bearing
credit-card balance to build credit. Strong credit history develops
through consistent behavior over time.

3.17 COMMON CREDIT MYTHS Checking your own report does not hurt your
score. Carrying a balance is not required to build credit. Closing a
card does not automatically improve credit because it can reduce
available revolving credit. A single universal credit score does not
exist, and 30% utilization is not an absolute pass/fail boundary.

============================================================ 4.
BUDGETING & SPENDING
============================================================

4.1 WHAT IS A BUDGET? A budget is a plan for income, spending, saving,
and debt payments over a period of time. It helps show whether current
spending fits available income and priorities.

4.2 INCOME VS. EXPENSES Income is money coming in; expenses are money
going out. When income exceeds expenses, the difference can be saved,
invested, or used for goals and debt. When expenses repeatedly exceed
income, savings or borrowing must cover the gap.

4.3 FIXED EXPENSES Fixed expenses are relatively predictable recurring
costs such as rent or a fixed loan payment. They are not always
permanently fixed, but they are usually easier to forecast than variable
spending.

4.4 VARIABLE EXPENSES Variable expenses change from period to period.
Food, gasoline, entertainment, utilities, and shopping can fluctuate,
making them important categories to track.

4.5 NEEDS VS. WANTS Needs are expenses necessary for basic living or
obligations; wants improve comfort or enjoyment. The line can differ by
person and circumstance. The goal is not to shame spending but to
understand tradeoffs.

4.6 BUILDING A MONTHLY BUDGET List reliable monthly income, required
bills, typical variable expenses, debt payments, savings, and irregular
costs. If totals do not fit, adjust categories or goals until the plan
is realistic.

4.7 TRACKING SPENDING Tracking transactions reveals where money actually
goes instead of relying on memory. Grouping purchases into categories
can expose patterns and make future budgets more accurate.

4.8 CASH FLOW Cash flow is the movement of money in and out. Positive
monthly cash flow means inflows exceed outflows; negative cash flow
means the opposite. Timing matters because a person can have enough
monthly income overall but still face a shortage before a bill is due.

4.9 SAVINGS RATE A simple savings-rate measure is amount saved divided
by income for the same period. Different tools define income and savings
differently, so PennyWise should show its formula rather than present
one percentage as universally correct.

4.10 LIVING WITHIN YOUR INCOME Living within income means recurring
spending and obligations do not consistently require new debt or
depletion of savings. It can require changing spending, increasing
income, or both.

4.11 LIFESTYLE INFLATION Lifestyle inflation occurs when spending rises
as income rises, leaving little improvement in saving capacity. A raise
can support both a better lifestyle and greater saving if the increase
is allocated intentionally.

4.12 IMPULSE SPENDING Impulse purchases are unplanned decisions made
with little deliberation. Waiting before nonessential purchases, using a
list, or setting a discretionary limit can create time to decide whether
the purchase fits current priorities.

4.13 SUBSCRIPTION SPENDING Small recurring charges can accumulate.
Review statements for services you no longer use, duplicate
subscriptions, trial conversions, and price increases.

4.14 ADJUSTING A BUDGET WHEN INCOME CHANGES When income falls,
prioritize essential expenses and required obligations and revisit
optional spending. When income rises, decide in advance how much goes to
current spending, savings, debt, or investing rather than allowing every
category to rise automatically.

============================================================ 5. SAVING &
EMERGENCY FUNDS
============================================================

5.1 WHY SAVE MONEY? Savings provide money for future purchases and
unexpected costs without immediately relying on debt. Savings can also
create flexibility during income disruptions.

5.2 SHORT-TERM VS. LONG-TERM SAVINGS Short-term savings are for goals
expected relatively soon and are often kept in liquid, lower-risk
accounts. Long-term goals may allow a different balance of liquidity and
investment risk depending on time horizon.

5.3 EMERGENCY FUNDS An emergency fund is money reserved for unexpected
necessary expenses or income loss. It is commonly held somewhere safe
and accessible, such as an insured savings account.

5.4 WHAT COUNTS AS AN EMERGENCY? Examples can include an essential car
repair, urgent medical cost, necessary home repair, or unexpected loss
of income. A planned vacation or routine bill is usually better handled
as a separate savings goal.

5.5 ESSENTIAL MONTHLY EXPENSES Essential expenses can include housing,
basic food, utilities, transportation needed for work, insurance,
minimum debt obligations, and necessary health costs. PennyWise can
total these to estimate how long available savings might cover
necessities.

5.6 CHOOSING AN EMERGENCY-FUND TARGET There is no single amount
appropriate for everyone. A user can choose a target based on essential
expenses, job stability, dependents, insurance, access to support, and
comfort with risk. PennyWise should let the user explore several target
sizes instead of declaring one mandatory number.

5.7 SETTING SAVINGS GOALS Define the amount, target date, current
balance, and expected contributions. Required monthly savings can be
estimated as remaining amount divided by remaining months, ignoring
interest for a simple conservative calculation.

5.8 AUTOMATING SAVINGS Automatic transfers can move part of each
paycheck or scheduled amount into savings before it is spent. Users
should choose an amount that does not cause overdrafts or missed bills.

5.9 SAVING WITH IRREGULAR INCOME When income varies, base essential
spending on a conservative income estimate and save more during stronger
months. Separating money for taxes may also be important for
self-employed or gig workers.

5.10 INTEREST AND APY Interest is money paid for the use of funds. For
deposit accounts, APY reflects the annualized yield including
compounding assumptions and helps compare accounts more consistently.

5.11 COMPOUND GROWTH Compounding means returns can be earned on the
original amount and on prior returns. Time, contribution size, return,
and fees all affect future value. Investment returns are uncertain;
savings-account rates can also change.

5.12 SAVING FOR LARGE PURCHASES Estimate the full cost, desired purchase
date, current savings, and monthly contribution. Saving before buying
can reduce how much must be financed and lets users see the tradeoff
between buying sooner and borrowing more.

============================================================ 6. YOUR
PAYCHECK & EMPLOYMENT
============================================================

6.1 UNDERSTANDING YOUR PAYCHECK A pay statement usually shows gross pay,
taxes withheld, benefit deductions, other deductions, and net pay.
Reviewing it helps verify hours, pay rate, and deductions.

6.2 GROSS PAY Gross pay is earnings before payroll deductions. For
hourly workers it generally reflects eligible hours times pay rates,
including applicable overtime; salaried pay is allocated according to
the employer’s pay schedule.

6.3 NET PAY Net pay is the amount remaining after taxes and other
deductions. It is the amount actually delivered by check or direct
deposit.

6.4 PAY PERIODS Employers may pay weekly, biweekly, semimonthly,
monthly, or on another lawful schedule. Biweekly means every two weeks
and normally creates 26 pay periods in a year; semimonthly usually means
twice a month, or 24 pay periods.

6.5 FEDERAL INCOME-TAX WITHHOLDING Employers generally withhold federal
income tax from employee wages. The amount depends on pay and
information provided on Form W-4, among other factors. Withholding is a
prepayment toward tax liability, not a separate extra tax.

6.6 STATE & LOCAL TAXES State and local income-tax rules depend on where
a person lives and works. Some states have no individual income tax
while others do, and some localities impose additional taxes. PennyWise
should use location-specific official sources rather than one nationwide
rule.

6.7 SOCIAL SECURITY TAX Social Security payroll tax helps fund Social
Security. Employees generally see an employee share withheld from
covered wages, while employers generally pay a corresponding employer
share. Rules and wage limits can change by year.

6.8 MEDICARE TAX Medicare payroll tax helps fund Medicare. Employees
generally have Medicare tax withheld from covered wages, and additional
Medicare tax rules can apply above statutory thresholds.

6.9 FORM W-4 Employees give Form W-4 to their employer so the employer
can calculate federal income-tax withholding. Life changes or a
significant mismatch between expected tax and withholding may justify
reviewing the form.

6.10 FORM W-2 Employers issue Form W-2 to report wages and certain taxes
withheld for an employee. Workers commonly use it when preparing a
federal tax return.

6.11 EMPLOYEE VS. INDEPENDENT CONTRACTOR Employees and independent
contractors are treated differently for tax and labor purposes.
Classification depends on the facts and law, not merely what a company
calls a worker. Contractors generally handle more of their own tax
payment responsibilities.

6.12 1099 / GIG WORK Certain nonemployee income may be reported on a
Form 1099. Even if a form is not received, taxable income may still need
to be reported. Self-employed workers may need to plan for income and
self-employment taxes and possible estimated payments.

6.13 EMPLOYEE BENEFITS Benefits can include health insurance, retirement
plans, paid leave, disability coverage, life insurance, education
assistance, and other programs. Compare both the value and the employee
cost, not salary alone.

6.14 HEALTH INSURANCE THROUGH WORK Employer plans may require employees
to pay part of the premium and may include deductibles, copays,
coinsurance, networks, and out-of-pocket limits. Compare total expected
cost and coverage rather than premium alone.

6.15 PAID TIME OFF Paid time off can include vacation, sick leave, or
combined PTO. Accrual, rollover, payout, and eligibility rules depend on
the employer and applicable law.

6.16 RETIREMENT BENEFITS Workplace plans such as a 401(k) can allow
workers to save for retirement with tax advantages. Investment options,
fees, withdrawal rules, and employer contributions vary by plan.

6.17 EMPLOYER MATCHING Some employers contribute when an employee
contributes to a retirement plan. The match formula and vesting rules
are plan-specific. Employees are always vested in their own 401(k)
contributions, but employer contributions may vest over time.

============================================================ 7. TAXES
============================================================

7.1 WHY TAXES EXIST Governments collect taxes to fund public programs
and services. In the U.S., taxes can exist at federal, state, and local
levels.

7.2 FEDERAL INCOME TAX Federal income tax is based on taxable income
under federal law. The U.S. uses tax brackets, meaning different
portions of taxable income can be taxed at different marginal rates.

7.3 STATE INCOME TAX State income-tax systems vary. Some states impose
graduated or flat individual income taxes and some impose none. Always
use the current rules for the relevant state.

7.4 LOCAL TAXES Cities, counties, school districts, or other local
governments may impose income, wage, property, sales, or other taxes
depending on jurisdiction.

7.5 SOCIAL SECURITY & MEDICARE TAXES Payroll taxes for Social Security
and Medicare are distinct from federal income tax. Employees commonly
see them separately on pay statements.

7.6 TAX WITHHOLDING Withholding sends part of an employee’s pay to tax
authorities during the year. Too little can result in tax due and
possibly penalties; too much can produce a refund, but a refund
generally means more was paid in than final liability required.

7.7 WHAT IS A TAX RETURN? A tax return reports relevant income,
deductions, credits, payments, and tax calculations for a tax year.
Filing requirements depend on factors such as income, filing status,
age, and type of income.

7.8 FORM 1040 Form 1040 is the primary U.S. individual federal
income-tax return. Schedules and additional forms may be required
depending on the taxpayer’s situation.

7.9 UNDERSTANDING YOUR W-2 A W-2 reports wages and certain federal,
Social Security, Medicare, and possibly state/local tax information.
Check that identifying information and wage amounts appear correct
before filing.

7.10 UNDERSTANDING 1099 FORMS Forms 1099 report various types of income
or transactions, such as nonemployee compensation, interest, dividends,
or certain payment-platform transactions. Different 1099 forms serve
different purposes.

7.11 W-2 VS. 1099 INCOME W-2 generally indicates employee wages with
employer withholding. 1099 income can include nonemployee or other
income where withholding may be limited or absent. The form alone does
not override the legal rules for worker classification.

7.12 TAX DEDUCTIONS A deduction generally reduces income subject to tax
when allowed. Its tax effect depends on the taxpayer’s situation and
applicable rules.

7.13 TAX CREDITS A tax credit generally reduces tax liability directly
when eligibility rules are met. Some credits can be refundable, meaning
part or all may be payable even if it exceeds tax otherwise owed.

7.14 STANDARD VS. ITEMIZED DEDUCTIONS Many taxpayers claim the standard
deduction; others itemize qualifying deductions when that produces a
better result or is required. Amounts and rules change, so PennyWise
should link to current IRS information.

7.15 TAX REFUNDS A refund generally occurs when payments and refundable
credits exceed final tax liability. A large refund is not automatically
“free money”; it may include money previously withheld from the
taxpayer’s income.

7.16 OWING TAXES If tax liability exceeds payments and credits, the
taxpayer may owe money. Filing on time and addressing the balance is
generally better than ignoring it; IRS payment options may be available.

7.17 FILING DEADLINES Federal individual returns are generally due in
the spring following the tax year, but the exact deadline can vary due
to weekends, holidays, disaster relief, or special circumstances. Show
the current IRS deadline rather than hard-coding a permanent date.

7.18 FILING AN EXTENSION An extension generally gives additional time to
file a federal return, not additional time to pay tax due. Estimate and
pay expected tax by the original payment deadline when required.

7.19 FILING TAXES FOR THE FIRST TIME Gather taxpayer identification
information, income forms, records for relevant deductions or credits,
and prior-year information if needed. Use reputable tax software, an
authorized preparer, or eligible free assistance.

7.20 FREE TAX-FILING RESOURCES The IRS offers Free File options for
eligible taxpayers and supports programs such as VITA and TCE for
qualifying populations. Eligibility and service availability can change
annually.

7.21 TAX SCAMS The IRS does not demand unusual payment methods such as
gift cards. Be suspicious of threats, urgent demands, or messages asking
for sensitive data. Verify tax communications through official IRS
channels.

7.22 ITIN VS. SSN FOR TAX PURPOSES An SSN is issued by the Social
Security Administration to eligible individuals. An ITIN is issued by
the IRS for federal tax purposes to certain people who need a taxpayer
identification number but are not eligible for an SSN. An ITIN does not
itself grant work authorization or immigration status.

============================================================ 8. DEBT &
LOANS ============================================================

8.1 WHAT IS DEBT? Debt is money or another obligation owed to someone
else. Common consumer debt includes credit cards, student loans, auto
loans, mortgages, and personal loans.

8.2 “GOOD” VS. “BAD” DEBT Calling debt good or bad can oversimplify it.
Evaluate purpose, interest rate, fees, affordability, repayment period,
risk, and alternatives. Even debt used for education or a home can
become harmful if the terms or payment burden are unsustainable.

8.3 PRINCIPAL Principal is the amount borrowed before interest and
certain charges. Loan payments may be divided between principal and
interest.

8.4 INTEREST Interest is the price paid for borrowing money. The amount
paid depends on rate, balance, time, payment schedule, and calculation
method.

8.5 APR APR is an annualized measure designed to help compare borrowing
costs. What APR includes can depend on the product and law, so users
should also inspect fees and total payment information.

8.6 LOAN TERMS Loan terms include amount financed, rate or APR, length,
payment amount, fees, collateral, late-payment rules, and prepayment
conditions. A lower monthly payment can still produce a higher total
cost if the term is longer.

8.7 MONTHLY PAYMENTS A loan payment may include principal, interest, and
sometimes other charges. Affordability should be judged alongside total
cost, not payment alone.

8.8 SECURED VS. UNSECURED LOANS Secured loans are backed by collateral
that the lender may be able to take after default, such as a vehicle or
home. Unsecured loans do not rely on specific collateral in the same
way, but nonpayment can still cause collections, credit damage, or legal
consequences.

8.9 PERSONAL LOANS Personal loans often provide a lump sum repaid in
installments. Compare APR, origination fees, repayment term, late fees,
and whether the rate is fixed or variable.

8.10 STUDENT LOANS Federal and private student loans have different
terms and protections. Federal loans can provide repayment options and
benefits not necessarily available from private lenders. Compare the
full terms before borrowing.

8.11 AUTO LOANS Auto loans finance a vehicle and are generally secured
by it. Compare vehicle price, down payment, APR, term, monthly payment,
add-ons, and total amount paid.

8.12 CREDIT-CARD DEBT Credit-card balances can carry relatively high
interest rates. Continuing to charge while making only minimum payments
can extend repayment substantially.

8.13 MINIMUM PAYMENTS The minimum is the smallest required monthly
payment under the account terms. Paying more than the minimum generally
reduces repayment time and interest when there is no prepayment penalty.

8.14 HOW INTEREST ADDS UP Interest increases the cost of borrowing over
time. Longer terms and higher rates usually increase total interest,
even when they make the monthly payment look smaller.

8.15 DEBT SNOWBALL The snowball strategy prioritizes the smallest
balance while making required payments on other debts. It can create
motivational wins but may cost more interest than prioritizing the
highest rate.

8.16 DEBT AVALANCHE The avalanche strategy prioritizes the
highest-interest debt while making required payments on the others.
Mathematically, it generally minimizes interest when payment amounts and
other assumptions are equal.

8.17 REFINANCING Refinancing replaces existing debt with new debt. It
may lower a rate or change the payment, but fees, a longer term, lost
protections, or variable rates can make a seemingly lower payment more
expensive overall.

8.18 CO-SIGNERS A co-signer agrees to be legally responsible for debt if
the primary borrower does not pay. The account can affect the
co-signer’s credit and borrowing capacity, so co-signing is a
significant financial commitment.

8.19 PREDATORY-LENDING WARNING SIGNS Warning signs can include extremely
high costs, pressure to act immediately, confusing terms, repeated
refinancing, or lending structured around inability to repay. Compare
alternatives and read required disclosures before signing.

============================================================ 9. RENTING
& HOUSING ============================================================

9.1 RENTING YOUR FIRST APARTMENT Estimate rent plus utilities,
insurance, transportation, deposits, application costs, and moving
expenses. Verify the property and landlord before paying or sharing
sensitive information.

9.2 UNDERSTANDING A LEASE A lease is a legal agreement describing rent,
term, deposits, responsibilities, rules, fees, and procedures for ending
or renewing tenancy. Read the full document before signing and keep a
copy.

9.3 APPLICATION FEES Landlords may charge application or screening fees
where permitted. Rules vary by state and locality. Confirm the property
is legitimate before paying.

9.4 SECURITY DEPOSITS A security deposit is money held under the lease
and applicable law to cover certain unpaid obligations or damage.
Limits, handling, deductions, and return deadlines depend on state/local
law.

9.5 MONTHLY RENT Rent is only one part of housing cost. Ask which
utilities, parking, internet, trash, amenities, or other charges are
included.

9.6 UTILITIES Utilities can include electricity, gas, water, sewer,
trash, and internet. Ask which accounts must be opened in your name and
whether deposits are required.

9.7 RENTERS INSURANCE Renters insurance can cover personal property and
liability subject to policy terms, limits, exclusions, and deductibles.
A landlord’s property insurance generally does not replace coverage for
a tenant’s belongings.

9.8 ROOMMATES Roommates can reduce housing costs but create shared
financial obligations. Understand whether tenants are jointly
responsible for the entire rent, how utilities are split, and what
happens if someone leaves.

9.9 CO-SIGNERS / GUARANTORS A landlord may request a guarantor when an
applicant lacks income or credit history. The guarantor can become
financially responsible under the agreement, so everyone should
understand the legal obligation.

9.10 CREDIT CHECKS FOR APARTMENTS Landlords may use consumer reports
where permitted. Applicants with limited U.S. credit history can ask
what alternative documentation the landlord accepts, such as proof of
income or a guarantor.

9.11 MOVING COSTS Budget for deposits, first rent payment,
transportation, furniture, utility setup, insurance, and supplies. A
move that appears affordable based on rent alone may require significant
upfront cash.

9.12 RENT AFFORDABILITY There is no universal rent percentage that fits
everyone. Compare housing cost with take-home income, debt,
transportation, essential expenses, savings, and local costs.

9.13 BUYING VS. RENTING Buying can build equity but involves transaction
costs, maintenance, taxes, insurance, and market risk. Renting can offer
flexibility and fewer ownership responsibilities. The better fit depends
on finances, location, time horizon, and preferences.

9.14 BASICS OF BUYING A HOME Homebuying commonly involves budgeting,
credit review, preapproval, property search, offer, inspection, loan
underwriting, appraisal, insurance, and closing. HUD-approved housing
counseling can help consumers understand the process.

9.15 MORTGAGES A mortgage is a loan secured by real property. Compare
interest rate, APR, fixed versus adjustable terms, loan length, fees,
mortgage insurance if applicable, and total projected payments.

9.16 DOWN PAYMENTS A down payment is money paid upfront toward the
purchase price. Required amounts vary by loan program and borrower
circumstances. A larger down payment can reduce the amount borrowed but
also uses cash that might otherwise remain available.

9.17 MORTGAGE INTEREST Mortgage interest is part of the cost of
borrowing. Small rate differences can have large long-term effects
because mortgages often involve large balances and long terms.

9.18 PROPERTY TAXES Property taxes are imposed by local governments and
can change over time. They may be paid directly or through a mortgage
escrow account.

9.19 HOMEOWNERS INSURANCE Homeowners insurance can cover specified
property damage and liability subject to policy terms. Mortgage lenders
generally require adequate coverage. Flood and earthquake risks may
require separate coverage.

9.20 CLOSING COSTS Closing costs can include lender charges, title
services, appraisal, recording fees, prepaid taxes or insurance, and
other expenses. Review the official loan disclosures rather than
focusing only on the down payment.

============================================================ 10. BUYING
& OWNING A CAR
============================================================

10.1 BUYING YOUR FIRST CAR Set a total budget before shopping and
include insurance, registration, taxes, fuel, maintenance, and
financing—not just sticker price.

10.2 NEW VS. USED New vehicles may include newer technology and
warranties but often cost more and can depreciate quickly. Used vehicles
may cost less but can carry greater maintenance uncertainty. Compare
total expected ownership cost.

10.3 VEHICLE PRICE VS. TOTAL COST The negotiated price is only one
component. Taxes, registration, financing charges, insurance, fuel,
maintenance, repairs, and optional products can substantially change
total cost.

10.4 DOWN PAYMENTS A down payment reduces the amount financed. More
money down can reduce monthly payment and interest, but buyers should
consider whether using too much cash would weaken emergency savings.

10.5 AUTO LOANS Shop for financing before visiting a dealer when
possible. Compare APR, term, amount financed, monthly payment, and total
of payments.

10.6 APR Auto-loan APR helps compare annualized borrowing costs. A lower
APR generally reduces financing cost, all else equal.

10.7 LOAN LENGTH Longer terms usually lower monthly payments but can
increase total interest and keep the borrower in debt longer. They can
also increase the period during which the loan balance exceeds the car’s
market value.

10.8 MONTHLY PAYMENTS Do not negotiate solely around a target monthly
payment. A dealer can lower the payment by extending the term while
increasing total cost.

10.9 DEALER FINANCING Dealers may arrange financing through lenders.
Compare the dealer’s offer with bank or credit-union preapproval and
review optional add-ons separately.

10.10 CAR INSURANCE Auto insurance requirements and prices vary by
state, driver, vehicle, coverage, deductible, and insurer. Obtain quotes
before committing to a vehicle.

10.11 REGISTRATION Vehicles generally must be titled and registered
according to state law. Fees and renewal schedules vary by state.

10.12 MAINTENANCE Oil, tires, brakes, inspections, and repairs are part
of ownership cost. A monthly maintenance reserve can make irregular
expenses easier to handle.

10.13 FUEL Fuel cost depends on efficiency, mileage driven, and fuel
prices. Compare expected annual fuel use rather than assuming two
similarly priced vehicles cost the same to operate.

10.14 DEPRECIATION Depreciation is the decline in a vehicle’s market
value over time. It is a real ownership cost even though it does not
appear as a monthly bill.

10.15 LEASING VS. BUYING A lease generally provides use of a vehicle for
a defined period with mileage and condition requirements; ownership does
not automatically transfer. Buying builds ownership as the loan is
repaid. Compare upfront cost, monthly payments, mileage needs,
end-of-term charges, and long-term plans.

10.16 TOTAL COST OF OWNERSHIP Add purchase price, financing, insurance,
taxes, registration, fuel, maintenance, repairs, and expected
depreciation. PennyWise’s simulator should show these components
separately.

============================================================ 11.
INSURANCE ============================================================

11.1 WHAT IS INSURANCE? Insurance transfers specified financial risks to
an insurer in exchange for premiums. Coverage only applies according to
the policy’s terms, exclusions, limits, and conditions.

11.2 PREMIUMS A premium is the price paid for insurance coverage. A
lower premium does not automatically mean a better policy because
deductibles, limits, networks, and exclusions may differ.

11.3 DEDUCTIBLES A deductible is an amount the insured may need to pay
before certain coverage begins. The way deductibles apply differs by
policy type.

11.4 COPAYS A copay is a fixed amount a health plan may require for a
covered service, such as an office visit or prescription. Details vary
by plan.

11.5 COINSURANCE Coinsurance is a percentage of covered costs the
insured pays after applicable deductible rules. For example, 20%
coinsurance means the member pays 20% of the allowed covered cost under
the plan’s rules.

11.6 COVERAGE LIMITS A coverage limit is the maximum an insurer will pay
under specified coverage. Higher limits can provide more protection but
may cost more.

11.7 HEALTH INSURANCE Health insurance helps pay covered medical costs.
Compare premiums, deductible, copays, coinsurance, network, prescription
coverage, and out-of-pocket maximum.

11.8 EMPLOYER HEALTH INSURANCE Employers may subsidize premiums and
offer several plan choices. Enrollment windows and qualifying life-event
rules matter. Compare the employee’s share of premiums and expected
out-of-pocket costs.

11.9 AUTO INSURANCE Auto policies can include liability, collision,
comprehensive, uninsured/underinsured motorist, medical-related
coverage, and other protections depending on state and policy. State
minimum coverage may not equal the amount of protection a household
wants.

11.10 RENTERS INSURANCE Renters coverage can protect belongings and
provide liability coverage under policy terms. Inventorying possessions
can help estimate needed coverage and document losses.

11.11 HOMEOWNERS INSURANCE Homeowners policies can cover a dwelling,
belongings, liability, and certain additional living expenses, subject
to terms. Standard policies do not cover every hazard.

11.12 LIFE INSURANCE BASICS Life insurance pays a death benefit to
beneficiaries when policy requirements are met. Term insurance covers a
specified period; permanent policies can include additional features and
generally have different costs and complexity.

11.13 DISABILITY INSURANCE BASICS Disability insurance can replace part
of income when a covered disability prevents work. Understand the
benefit amount, waiting period, duration, definition of disability,
exclusions, and employer versus individual coverage.

11.14 COMPARING POLICIES Compare equivalent coverage, not premium alone.
Review deductibles, limits, exclusions, networks, riders, claims
process, and insurer information.

============================================================ 12.
INVESTING ============================================================

12.1 SAVING VS. INVESTING Saving usually emphasizes safety and liquidity
for near-term needs. Investing puts money into assets such as stocks or
bonds with the expectation of return but involves risk of loss.

12.2 WHY PEOPLE INVEST People invest to pursue long-term goals such as
retirement, education, or wealth building. Returns can come from price
appreciation, interest, or dividends, but none is guaranteed for market
investments.

12.3 RISK & RETURN All investments involve risk. Investments with
greater potential return can also expose investors to greater loss or
volatility. Time horizon and ability to tolerate loss matter.

12.4 COMPOUND GROWTH Compound growth occurs when returns are earned on
both original contributions and prior returns. Starting earlier gives
compounding more time, but hypothetical growth examples are not
guarantees.

12.5 STOCKS A stock represents an ownership interest in a company. Share
prices can rise or fall, and companies may or may not pay dividends.

12.6 BONDS A bond is generally debt issued by a government,
municipality, or company. Investors lend money in exchange for promised
interest and repayment terms, but bonds still carry risks such as
credit, interest-rate, and inflation risk.

12.7 ETFs An exchange-traded fund pools investor money into a portfolio
and trades on an exchange. ETFs can hold stocks, bonds, or other assets.
Diversification, fees, strategy, and risk vary widely.

12.8 MUTUAL FUNDS A mutual fund pools money from many investors into a
portfolio managed according to a stated objective. Investors should
review fees, strategy, holdings, and risks.

12.9 INDEX FUNDS An index fund seeks to track a market index rather than
selecting securities solely to outperform it. Index funds can be mutual
funds or ETFs and still involve market risk and fees.

12.10 DIVIDENDS Dividends are distributions a company or fund may make
to investors. They are not guaranteed and can be reduced or stopped.

12.11 CAPITAL GAINS A capital gain generally occurs when an investment
is sold for more than its tax basis; a loss can occur when sold for
less. Tax treatment depends on holding period, account type, and current
tax law.

12.12 BROKERAGE ACCOUNTS A brokerage account allows investors to buy and
sell securities. Compare fees, investment choices, cash protections,
trading features, and whether the brokerage is properly registered.

12.13 DIVERSIFICATION Diversification spreads money across investments
to reduce concentration risk. It can reduce the damage from one holding
performing poorly, but it cannot guarantee against market losses.

12.14 ASSET ALLOCATION Asset allocation divides investments among
categories such as stocks, bonds, and cash. The appropriate mix depends
on goals, time horizon, and risk tolerance rather than a universal
formula.

12.15 RISK TOLERANCE Risk tolerance describes how much investment
uncertainty or loss an investor is willing and able to accept. Emotional
comfort and financial capacity are both relevant.

12.16 TIME HORIZON Time horizon is how long until money is expected to
be needed. Longer horizons may provide more time to recover from market
declines; short-term money generally requires greater attention to
liquidity and loss risk.

12.17 MARKET VOLATILITY Volatility describes how much prices fluctuate.
Temporary declines are normal in markets, but some investments can
experience permanent losses.

12.18 DOLLAR-COST AVERAGING Dollar-cost averaging means investing equal
amounts at regular intervals regardless of market price. It can create
discipline but does not guarantee profit or protect against loss.

12.19 INVESTMENT FEES Fees reduce investment returns. Examples include
expense ratios, advisory fees, commissions, account fees, and certain
transaction costs.

12.20 EXPENSE RATIOS An expense ratio represents annual operating
expenses of a fund as a percentage of assets. Small percentage
differences can compound into meaningful dollar differences over long
periods.

12.21 ACTIVE VS. PASSIVE INVESTING Active strategies attempt to select
or trade investments to outperform a benchmark or meet another
objective. Passive strategies often seek to track an index. Both can
involve fees and risk.

12.22 INVESTMENT SCAMS Warning signs include guaranteed high returns,
pressure to act immediately, secrecy, unregistered sellers, and requests
to send money through unusual methods. Verify investment professionals
and offerings through official regulatory resources.

12.23 CRYPTO — UNDERSTANDING THE RISKS Crypto assets can be highly
volatile and may lack protections associated with traditional insured
deposits or registered securities products. FDIC insurance does not
insure crypto assets. Users should understand custody, fraud, platform,
technology, and price risks before considering them.

============================================================ 13.
RETIREMENT ============================================================

13.1 WHAT IS RETIREMENT PLANNING? Retirement planning means building
financial resources for years when employment income may decrease or
stop. It can involve workplace plans, IRAs, Social Security, personal
savings, and other assets.

13.2 WHY STARTING EARLIER MATTERS Earlier contributions have more time
for potential compound growth. Starting later does not make retirement
saving pointless, but reaching the same goal can require larger
contributions.

13.3 COMPOUND GROWTH Reinvested earnings can generate additional
earnings over time. PennyWise projections should label return
assumptions as hypothetical and show that actual markets vary.

13.4 EMPLOYER RETIREMENT PLANS Common defined-contribution plans include
401(k), 403(b), and certain 457 plans. Rules, investment choices, fees,
employer contributions, and withdrawal provisions vary.

13.5 WHAT IS A 401(K)? A 401(k) is an employer-sponsored retirement plan
that allows eligible employees to contribute from pay, subject to tax
rules and annual limits. Contributions are then invested in options
offered by the plan.

13.6 EMPLOYER MATCHING Some employers match a portion of employee
contributions. Match formulas vary. Review the plan document to
understand how much an employee must contribute to receive the available
match.

13.7 TRADITIONAL 401(K) Traditional 401(k) employee contributions are
generally made on a pre-tax basis for federal income-tax purposes, and
qualified withdrawals are generally taxable. Specific tax consequences
depend on current law and individual circumstances.

13.8 ROTH 401(K) Designated Roth 401(k) contributions are made after
tax. Qualified distributions can be tax-free if legal requirements are
satisfied.

13.9 WHAT IS AN IRA? An Individual Retirement Arrangement is a
tax-advantaged retirement account established by an individual rather
than an employer. Eligibility, deductibility, and contribution rules
depend on law and circumstances.

13.10 TRADITIONAL IRA Traditional IRA contributions may be deductible
depending on income, filing status, and workplace-plan coverage.
Earnings generally grow tax-deferred, and taxable distributions are
generally included in income.

13.11 ROTH IRA Roth IRA contributions are made with after-tax dollars
and are subject to income and contribution rules. Qualified
distributions can be tax-free.

13.12 TRADITIONAL VS. ROTH The major distinction is generally the timing
of tax treatment: traditional arrangements may provide tax benefits on
contributions under applicable rules with taxes later, while Roth
contributions are after-tax with qualified withdrawals potentially
tax-free. Individual circumstances determine which features are more
valuable.

13.13 CONTRIBUTION LIMITS Retirement-plan and IRA contribution limits
can change each year. PennyWise should retrieve or display the current
IRS limit with a year label rather than permanently embedding one
amount.

13.14 VESTING Vesting determines ownership of employer-provided
retirement contributions. Employees are always fully vested in their own
401(k) contributions, while employer contributions may vest under the
plan’s schedule.

13.15 RETIREMENT WITHDRAWALS Retirement accounts have rules for
distributions, taxes, and possible penalties. Exceptions and
required-distribution rules can change, so users should consult current
IRS guidance before withdrawing.

13.16 ROLLOVER BASICS A rollover moves eligible retirement money from
one retirement arrangement to another. Direct rollovers can avoid
certain withholding complications, but account type and tax treatment
matter.

13.17 SOCIAL SECURITY BASICS Social Security can provide retirement,
disability, and survivor benefits to eligible people based on program
rules and work history. It is separate from a 401(k) or IRA and should
not be described as a personal investment account.

============================================================ 14. COLLEGE
& EDUCATION ============================================================

14.1 UNDERSTANDING COLLEGE COSTS College cost includes more than
tuition. Consider fees, housing, food, books, supplies, transportation,
and personal expenses, then subtract grants and scholarships to estimate
net cost.

14.2 TUITION VS. TOTAL COST Tuition is the charge for instruction; total
cost of attendance is broader and can include living and educational
expenses. Comparing schools by tuition alone can be misleading.

14.3 FAFSA BASICS The Free Application for Federal Student Aid is used
to apply for federal student aid and can also be used by states and
schools. Students should use the official Federal Student Aid process
and submit required information accurately.

14.4 GRANTS Grants are financial aid that generally does not have to be
repaid, although exceptions can apply if eligibility conditions are not
met. Federal, state, institutional, and private grants have different
rules.

14.5 SCHOLARSHIPS Scholarships are funds awarded under criteria set by
the provider and generally do not require repayment. Never assume a
scholarship is legitimate if it requires suspicious upfront payments or
sensitive information without verification.

14.6 FEDERAL STUDENT LOANS Federal student loans are funded or
guaranteed under federal programs and have standardized terms and
federal protections. Borrow only what is needed and review interest,
fees, and repayment options.

14.7 PRIVATE STUDENT LOANS Private education loans come from banks,
credit unions, or other private lenders. Rates, co-signer requirements,
repayment options, and borrower protections can differ substantially
from federal loans.

14.8 SUBSIDIZED VS. UNSUBSIDIZED For eligible Direct Subsidized Loans,
the federal government pays interest during certain qualifying periods.
Direct Unsubsidized Loans accrue interest during all periods, including
while the borrower is in school.

14.9 STUDENT-LOAN INTEREST Interest increases the amount repaid.
Understand whether interest accrues during school, whether unpaid
interest can be capitalized under applicable rules, and the loan’s fixed
or variable rate.

14.10 REPAYMENT BASICS Repayment plans determine monthly payments and
timeline. Federal borrowers may have multiple plan options subject to
current rules; private-loan options depend on the lender.

14.11 WORK-STUDY Federal Work-Study provides part-time employment to
eligible students with financial need at participating schools. An award
does not mean money is automatically credited like a grant; students
generally earn wages by working.

14.12 EDUCATION SAVINGS BASICS Families may use savings accounts or
tax-advantaged education accounts such as 529 plans, subject to
applicable rules. Investment risk, fees, tax benefits, and qualified-use
requirements vary.

============================================================ 15.
FINANCIAL GOALS
============================================================

15.1 SETTING FINANCIAL GOALS A useful goal states what you want, how
much it costs, when you want it, and what you can contribute. Specific
numbers make progress measurable.

15.2 SHORT-TERM GOALS Short-term goals are expected relatively soon,
such as a small emergency reserve or upcoming purchase. Liquidity and
principal stability can matter more when the money will be needed soon.

15.3 MEDIUM-TERM GOALS Medium-term goals might include a vehicle,
relocation, or larger emergency reserve. Choose savings or investment
risk based on how flexible the date is and how much loss you could
tolerate.

15.4 LONG-TERM GOALS Long-term goals such as retirement or a distant
home purchase may allow more time for investment growth and recovery
from volatility, but still require an appropriate risk plan.

15.5 PRIORITIZING MULTIPLE GOALS Separate urgent obligations from
flexible wants, then decide how available cash is divided. PennyWise can
let users change priorities and see how increasing one contribution
delays or accelerates another goal.

15.6 SAVING FOR A CAR Include down payment, taxes, registration,
insurance, and an initial repair or maintenance reserve. Compare the
effect of a larger down payment with keeping emergency savings.

15.7 SAVING FOR EDUCATION Estimate net education cost after expected
aid, then separate near-term tuition needs from longer-term savings.
Avoid assuming future scholarships or investment returns are guaranteed.

15.8 SAVING FOR A HOME A home goal can include down payment, closing
costs, moving expenses, initial repairs, and cash reserves. The down
payment alone is not the full upfront cost.

15.9 SAVING FOR TRAVEL Estimate transportation, lodging, food,
activities, insurance, local transportation, and currency costs. A
dedicated sinking fund can keep planned travel separate from emergency
savings.

15.10 SAVING FOR RETIREMENT Retirement is a long-term goal where time
and regular contributions can be powerful. Employer matches and
tax-advantaged accounts can be important, but investment outcomes remain
uncertain.

15.11 TRACKING PROGRESS Progress percentage can be calculated as current
saved amount divided by target amount. Show both dollars and percentage
so users understand what remains.

15.12 ADJUSTING GOALS Life changes can make a goal unrealistic or less
important. Users should be able to change amount, date, or contribution
without being told they “failed.”

============================================================ 16. FRAUD,
SCAMS & FINANCIAL SAFETY
============================================================

16.1 PROTECTING YOUR MONEY Slow down when someone creates urgency.
Verify the organization using contact information you independently know
is legitimate, and never share passwords or one-time security codes with
an unexpected caller or message.

16.2 PHISHING Phishing messages impersonate trusted organizations to
steal information or install malicious software. Avoid clicking
unexpected links; instead, open the official app or type the known
website yourself.

16.3 FAKE BANK MESSAGES Scammers can spoof bank names or phone numbers
and claim there is fraud on your account. A real-looking caller ID is
not proof. Contact your bank using the number on your card or official
app.

16.4 IDENTITY THEFT Identity theft occurs when someone uses personal
information without permission, often for financial gain. If it happens,
IdentityTheft.gov provides an FTC recovery process and personalized
steps.

16.5 CREDIT-CARD FRAUD Review transactions and report unauthorized
charges promptly to the issuer. Card networks and federal protections
can apply, but users should act quickly and follow the issuer’s dispute
process.

16.6 INVESTMENT SCAMS Be skeptical of guaranteed returns, little or no
risk, secret strategies, pressure, or unregistered sellers. Verify
investment professionals through official regulatory databases.

16.7 TAX SCAMS Scammers impersonate tax agencies and threaten arrest or
demand immediate payment. Verify claims directly with the IRS and never
pay a supposed tax debt using gift cards or other unusual methods.

16.8 JOB SCAMS Legitimate employers do not require applicants to pay to
get a job. Fake-check schemes often send a check and instruct the victim
to buy equipment or send money back; the check later fails and the
victim loses the real money sent.

16.9 RENTAL SCAMS Scammers copy legitimate listings or invent
properties, then demand deposits before a victim can verify the rental.
Search the address, verify the owner or company, and avoid payment
demands involving gift cards, crypto, or wire transfers.

16.10 ROMANCE SCAMS A scammer may build an online relationship and
eventually request money, investments, or financial help. Never send
money to someone you have only met online based on an urgent story.

16.11 MONEY-TRANSFER SCAMS Wire transfers, gift cards, and
cryptocurrency can be difficult to reverse. Treat requests for these
payment methods from strangers or unexpected contacts as major warning
signs.

16.12 PROTECTING YOUR SSN Share an SSN only when there is a legitimate
need and you understand who is requesting it and why. Avoid carrying
unnecessary documents containing the number and securely store records.

16.13 STRONG PASSWORDS & MFA Use long, unique passwords for financial
accounts and enable multifactor authentication where available. Reusing
passwords allows one breach to threaten multiple accounts.

16.14 FREEZING YOUR CREDIT A security freeze restricts access to a
credit file and can make it harder for identity thieves to open new
accounts. Freezes are handled separately with each nationwide
credit-reporting company and can be lifted when needed.

16.15 WHAT TO DO IF YOUR IDENTITY IS STOLEN Report identity theft at
IdentityTheft.gov, follow the recovery plan, contact affected companies,
review credit reports, and consider credit freezes or fraud alerts as
appropriate.

16.16 REPORTING FRAUD Report scams to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and
contact the financial company used to send money as quickly as possible
to ask whether a transaction can be stopped or reversed. Other reports
may be appropriate depending on the scam.

============================================================ 17. MONEY
FOR NEWCOMERS TO AMERICA
============================================================

17.1 NEW TO THE U.S.? START HERE Focus first on safe access to money,
income, housing, and protection from fraud. Learn how bank accounts,
paychecks, taxes, credit, and insurance work before moving into more
advanced investing topics.

17.2 UNDERSTANDING U.S. CURRENCY The U.S. dollar is divided into 100
cents. Cash remains legal tender, but many everyday payments use debit
cards, credit cards, bank transfers, or mobile payment services. Keep
receipts for important purchases and monitor electronic transactions.

17.3 OPENING YOUR FIRST U.S. BANK ACCOUNT Ask several banks or credit
unions what identification they accept because requirements can differ.
Compare fees, minimums, ATM access, direct deposit, transfer features,
and federal deposit insurance. Never pay a stranger to “guarantee” you a
bank account.

17.4 UNDERSTANDING SSNs & ITINs An SSN and ITIN are different
taxpayer-identification numbers issued by different agencies. An ITIN is
for federal tax processing for eligible people who cannot obtain an SSN;
it does not provide work authorization or change immigration status.

17.5 RECEIVING YOUR FIRST PAYCHECK Check gross pay, hours or salary,
taxes, benefit deductions, and net pay. If something appears wrong, ask
payroll or HR. Keep W-2s and other tax documents for filing.

17.6 ESTABLISHING U.S. CREDIT HISTORY A person can arrive with no U.S.
credit file even after responsibly managing money elsewhere. Start
slowly with products that report to U.S. credit bureaus, pay on time,
and avoid expensive products that promise instant credit improvement.

17.7 GETTING YOUR FIRST CREDIT CARD Compare annual fees, APR,
security-deposit requirements, credit limits, and reporting practices. A
secured card can be one option for limited credit history. Spending
small amounts and paying on time is more important than carrying debt.

17.8 RENTING WITHOUT ESTABLISHED CREDIT Ask the landlord what
alternatives are accepted, such as income verification, references, a
larger lawful deposit, or a guarantor. Requirements and deposit limits
vary by state and locality.

17.9 BUYING A CAR IN THE U.S. Compare insurance cost and financing
before choosing a car. Newcomers with limited U.S. credit may receive
different loan offers, making APR and total amount paid especially
important to compare.

17.10 UNDERSTANDING U.S. TAXES Employees may have taxes withheld from
pay, while self-employed workers may need to make their own payments.
Tax filing rules depend on income and tax status. Immigration status and
tax residency are legal concepts that should not be guessed by
PennyWise; direct users to IRS guidance or qualified help.

17.11 UNDERSTANDING HEALTH INSURANCE U.S. health care can involve
premiums, deductibles, copays, coinsurance, provider networks, and
out-of-pocket limits. Coverage may come from an employer, a government
program if eligible, or an individual marketplace. Eligibility rules can
depend on personal circumstances.

17.12 SENDING MONEY INTERNATIONALLY A remittance transfer sends money to
a recipient abroad. Compare exchange rate, transfer fee, amount
recipient receives, delivery time, and cancellation/error-resolution
rights when applicable. The cheapest advertised fee does not always
produce the most money for the recipient.

17.13 CURRENCY EXCHANGE Exchange rates determine how much foreign
currency is received for U.S. dollars or vice versa. Providers can earn
money through both explicit fees and the rate offered, so compare the
final amount received.

17.14 INTERNATIONAL TRANSFER FEES Possible costs include sender fees,
intermediary fees, recipient fees, and exchange-rate markup. Compare the
total delivered amount rather than one fee in isolation.

17.15 BUILDING AN EMERGENCY FUND Moving countries can create irregular
costs such as deposits, transportation, documents, or family
emergencies. Start with an achievable amount and increase it as finances
stabilize rather than treating one target as mandatory.

17.16 UNDERSTANDING RETIREMENT ACCOUNTS A U.S. employer may offer a
401(k) or similar plan, sometimes with matching contributions. IRAs are
individual retirement arrangements. Tax rules, withdrawal rules,
contribution limits, and eligibility matter, so read current plan and
IRS information.

17.17 FINANCIAL SCAMS TARGETING NEWCOMERS Scammers may exploit
unfamiliarity with U.S. institutions by impersonating government
agencies, employers, landlords, banks, or immigration-related
authorities. Urgent threats and demands for gift cards, cryptocurrency,
or wire transfers are strong warning signs. Verify independently before
paying or sharing information.

17.18 WHERE TO FIND TRUSTED FINANCIAL INFORMATION Use primary government
and regulatory sources when possible: - CFPB: consumer banking, credit,
loans, mortgages, debt, remittances - FDIC: insured bank deposits and
banking education - NCUA: federally insured credit unions - IRS: federal
taxes, W-4, W-2, ITINs, retirement tax rules - Investor.gov / SEC:
investing and fraud education - FTC: scams, identity theft, consumer
protection - Federal Student Aid: FAFSA, grants, federal loans,
work-study - Department of Labor: workplace retirement and benefit
information - HUD: housing counseling and homebuying resources
